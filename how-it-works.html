How It Works

Where the figures come from, what they count, and how to use them.

Where the figures come from

Every figure comes from the Home Office's small boat activity page on GOV.UK. We use two parts of it:

  • Last 7 days: updated every day, and the source of the newest figures.
  • Time series: daily figures back to 1 January 2018, updated on Fridays.

The Home Office describes these figures as provisional. Final, checked figures appear later in the quarterly Immigration System Statistics. Channel Crossings is independent and not linked to the UK Government.

When it updates

The site checks the Home Office page for new figures every 30 minutes through the day, from about 8am to 7:30pm UK time, so new or late figures (weekend figures sometimes arrive on Monday afternoon) usually appear within half an hour. The Home Office usually publishes a day's figure the following morning, so the latest day shown is normally yesterday. At weekends and on bank holidays the figures can arrive later.

What is counted

  • People: people detected arriving in the UK in small boats such as dinghies and inflatable boats, including those picked up at sea and brought ashore.
  • Boats: the number of small boats those people arrived in.
  • Uncontrolled landings: boats that reached the shore without being intercepted at sea. The Home Office only includes these in its daily page, so our count starts from 19 September 2026.

The figures leave out people who arrive hidden in vehicles or on larger vessels, and anyone who arrives undetected. Days with no crossings are usually down to bad weather.

Revised figures

The Home Office sometimes corrects a day's figure after first publishing it. When that happens, the site shows the new figure with a note such as Revised from 412, so changes are never made silently.

Totals, records and busy days

  • Year so far adds up every day since 1 January. The comparison with last year uses the same date range last year.
  • Records are worked out from the daily figures since 2018.
  • An orange dot marks one of the busiest 5% of days since 2018.
  • A blue dot marks figures that are new since your last visit. Your phone or computer remembers this, not us.

Since Labour and since Burnham

These totals add up every day's arrivals from a starting date: 5 July 2024, when Labour took office under Keir Starmer, and 20 July 2026, when Andy Burnham became Prime Minister. Both include the starting day.

Estimated annual taxpayer cost. Under the Labour total, the site shows a rough yearly cost: the number of people who have arrived since 5 July 2024, multiplied by the average cost of asylum support per person. That average is about £37,500 a year: the £4.0 billion the Home Office spent on asylum support in 2024–25 (National Audit Office, December 2025), divided by the 106,771 people receiving asylum support at the end of March 2025 (Home Office). It is an illustration, not an official figure: not everyone who arrives by small boat is receiving asylum support at any one time, and it leaves out other costs such as healthcare, education, legal aid and processing claims.

Sex and age

These come from the Home Office's quarterly Immigration System Statistics (dataset IER_D01), not the daily figures, so they update every three months. "Last year" covers the latest four quarters. Adult men are males aged 18 and over. People whose sex or age wasn't recorded are shown separately.

Petitions

The site lists open petitions about small boat crossings from the UK Parliament petitions site, plus any that closed in the past year, and checks for new ones daily. We don't create or endorse any petition.

The arrivals estimate

The estimate for today or tomorrow is a simple guide, not an official forecast. It rates the forecast weather for the Dover Strait as calm, moderate or rough, then looks at how many people arrived on days with the same rating over the last 120 days, or the last year if there were too few. The range shown covers the middle half of those days. After two or more rough days, a calm day is compared with other calm days that followed rough spells, because more people tend to cross then.

Weather forecasts change and many other things affect crossings, so real numbers can fall well outside the range.

Channel conditions

The weather notice uses Open-Meteo wind and wave forecasts for the middle of the Dover Strait. Crossings cluster on calm days, so the site rates conditions as calm (wind under 12 mph and waves under 0.5 m), moderate (wind under 18 mph and waves under 1 m) or rough.

This is a rough guide for context, not a marine forecast. Never use it to decide whether it is safe to go to sea.

Breaking news

Once an hour the site reads the public news feeds of BBC News, Sky News, The Guardian, The Telegraph, GB News and GOV.UK, and keeps headlines from the last three hours that are clearly about small boats or Channel crossings. The newest appears in a box at the top of the page with a link to the full story on the outlet's own site. Channel Crossings doesn't write or check these stories; each one is the responsibility of the outlet that published it.

Download the data

Download every day since 2018 as a CSV file. It has four columns: date, people, boats and uncontrolled landings. It's updated whenever new figures are published.
